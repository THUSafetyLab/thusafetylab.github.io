Human-centered intelligent systems research

Human–AI Interaction and Collaboration

Safe and effective intelligent systems depend on how people perceive hazards, intervene, learn when to rely on automation, and coordinate with AI. Our research combines driver-in-the-loop experiments, probabilistic response modeling, repeated human–AI decision tasks and social-norm learning to understand these interactions and support human-centered design.

Research framework

Objective

Understand how human behavior and AI capability jointly shape safety and collaboration. We examine when drivers can respond effectively, how people learn the limits of AI advice, and which social expectations enable compatible interaction. These complementary studies connect human behavioral evidence with system evaluation and decision design.
01
Socially compatible AI · Unpublished manuscript

Social norms for compatible human–AI coordination

We study how AI agents can coordinate with people in dynamic interactions, using pedestrian–vehicle encounters as a representative setting. This work examines how shared social expectations can be represented and incorporated into decision-making to support safe, predictable and mutually compatible behavior. The research aims to inform the design of intelligent agents that interact naturally with people.

02
Human–AI learning · Research in progress

Learning to collaborate with LLMs under time pressure

This ongoing project examines how people learn when to rely on AI advice under time pressure. Through repeated decision tasks, we investigate how interaction experience and perceived AI capability shape trust and reliance. The goal is to support collaboration in which human expectations remain aligned with the system’s capabilities and limitations.

03
Driver–automation interaction · AAP 2026

Driver intervention and automation safety boundaries

44 drivers · 2,582 simulator events~26% collision risk conditional on intervention
Simulator-based interaction data collection and safety evaluation across automation levels (Fig. 1d–e).

Automated-driving safety depends on how drivers interact with the system when hazards emerge. A driver-in-the-loop study collected 2,582 safety-critical events from 44 drivers across L0–L4. As automation capability increased, interventions became less frequent and generally later, while overall safety improved. However, collision probability conditional on intervention remained approximately 26% across L2–L4: 26.72%, 26.43% and 25.54%, respectively. Risk at intervention onset and response latency were closely associated with collision outcomes, revealing a broadly similar risk-based controllability boundary across levels. These simulator findings distinguish system capability from realized safety and show why initiating intervention alone does not guarantee successful hazard resolution.

Related study: Qin et al. (IET ITS 2026) modeled drivers' perception-response time across four pre-crash conflict types with a Bayesian framework conditioned on visual attention latency and scenario urgency, using 9,512 responses to 1,740 pre-crash videos; in naturalistic rear-end crashes with urgency of 0.75–1.85 s, its 50% predictive intervals covered 93.94% of cases, supporting driver-state-aware warning and intervention timing.
